* **Image painting / persistence-of-vision photography.** Take any image, resize it to 100 pixels tall, slice it into vertical columns, and transmit one column at a time while you sweep the wand across the camera frame during a long exposure. Your 3-second idea is exactly this. Portraits, typography, abstract art, photographs, gradients—even one of your own paintings could become floating light.
* **Deliberately distorted images.** Don’t try to move the wand perfectly. Let acceleration, hesitation, rotation, and wobble distort the source image. A face could stretch, fracture, compress, or ripple depending on how you move. That feels much more like *your* territory than merely reproducing an image accurately.
* **Procedural light painting.** Generate each 100-pixel frame live with Python rather than reading an image: Perlin/noise fields, cellular automata, reaction-diffusion, waves, interference patterns, Lissajous functions, Mandelbrot slices, strange attractors, etc. The photograph becomes the accumulated output of an algorithm.
* **The wand as a timeline.** Instead of thinking spatially, let color represent changing information over time. One sweep could visualize temperature, music frequency, stock prices, weather, a photograph's color palette, or practically any dataset.
* **Music painting.** Analyze music on the laptop and transmit FFT bands to the wand. Bass might occupy one end, treble the other, with brightness tied to amplitude. Long exposure photography would turn an entire phrase of music into a physical-looking waveform.
* **Generative calligraphy.** Send bands of light, moving points, flashes, and gradients while drawing gestures in space. You could develop a vocabulary of movements—slash, loop, spiral, orbit—and pair each gesture with particular light behavior.
* **Multiple-pass compositions.** Keep the camera shutter open and make several controlled passes: one creates a broad translucent field, another draws sharp lines, another adds isolated bright marks. Basically Photoshop layers performed physically.
* **Light masks.** Use mostly darkness with only a few LEDs illuminated. As the wand moves, those isolated LEDs become parallel lines. Animate their positions and you can create meshes, ribbons, cages, tunnels, topographic-looking structures, or DNA-like helices.
* **Virtual geometry.** Calculate the LED frames required to make circles, grids, cubes, spirals, or mathematical surfaces appear in the photograph as you move the wand. It would be fun to deliberately blur the boundary between calculated geometry and imperfect human movement.
* **Photographic interaction.** Point the camera at a person, tree, building, sculpture, abandoned object, etc., and use the wand to construct something around it—halos, force fields, geometric structures, flowing energy, outlines. The existing subject becomes an anchor rather than the whole image.
* **"Overlooked" light intervention.** This one connects nicely with your existing work. Photograph mundane utility objects at night—hydrants, meters, pipes, electrical boxes—and use the wand to visually reveal imagined flows of electricity, water, data, pressure, or energy around them.
* **Invisible sculpture.** Create patterns designed specifically for movement around an object. Walk a circle around a chair, tree, person, or sculpture while the LEDs change according to position. The final photograph shows a luminous volume that never physically existed.
* **Feedback loops.** Photograph a generated wand image, feed that photograph back into Python, transform it, then paint the transformed version again. After several generations you'd get a physical/digital mutation process very much like your Curiosity → Experiment → Mutation loop.
* **Controlled failure experiments.** Intentionally violate the assumptions: reverse direction halfway through, change transmission speed mid-sweep, rotate the wand, stop briefly, shake it, swing it as a pendulum, or walk toward the camera instead of sideways. Catalog what each "mistake" does visually.
* **Pendulum paintings.** Since you deliberately preserved that mounting possibility, this could be excellent. Suspend the wand and let physics determine its path while Python controls the light pattern. Change pendulum length, initial release angle, LED sequence, exposure length, or introduce a second axis. You're essentially combining harmonographs with programmable light.
* **Motion-reactive wand.** Once you eventually add the BNO085, things get especially interesting: color based on angular velocity, brightness based on acceleration, patterns triggered by direction changes, or image playback advancing according to actual distance traveled rather than elapsed time.

One idea I particularly like for you is **"motion corrupts the image."** Start with a recognizable portrait or photograph. Python sends the correct 100-pixel slices, but your imperfect physical movement becomes part of the rendering engine. Don't digitally correct it. The gaps, stretches and bends become evidence of the human hand.

That gets you away from *"Look, I built a POV display"* and toward **"I built a photographic instrument whose marks are produced jointly by code, movement, light and time."**

And I'd make your first serious experiment very small: choose one simple 100×100 image, shoot a 3-second exposure, sweep left-to-right, and send exactly 100 columns over those three seconds—**33.3 columns/sec, about 30 ms per column**. Then repeat it four times while deliberately changing only the wand movement: steady, accelerating, wavy, and stop/start.

That one experiment will probably teach you more about what this thing wants to become than another week of hardware tinkering.
